# Evidence

Hartbeat sells evidence-based HR, so every claim in the product needs a source that has been checked. Mark anything unverified as such.

## Wellbeing strategy

Main source: Qamar, F., Afshan, G., & Rana, S. A. (2024). Sustainable HRM and well-being: Systematic review and future research agenda. *Management Review Quarterly, 74*, 2289–2339. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11301-023-00360-6

A review of 134 studies. Its own caveat: most studies are conceptual or one-time surveys, so they show links, not proof of cause. Hence two evidence labels in the product ("Survey evidence", "Early evidence") and before/after measurement of every plan.

The study citations below were taken from the review's summary tables, not from the original papers. Check the originals before quoting them externally.

| Initiative | Route | Source (via the review) |
|---|---|---|

| Flexible working trial | Flexible working strengthens HR to wellbeing; flexitime, telework, compressed weeks with absence | Donald et al. 2020; Mariappanadar 2020 |

The three pulse items per survey are short Hartbeat versions inspired by these scales, not the validated wording. Before using a survey for decisions, use the validated items (with licences where needed).

## Recruiting

- Structured interviews and job knowledge predict performance best; years of experience alone predict little (Sackett et al. 2022). Verify figures before quoting.
- Conscientiousness predicts across most jobs; extraversion mainly in sales and management (Barrick & Mount 1991). Personality is capped at 10% and only from an optional validated questionnaire, never inferred from a CV.
- External hires paid more yet rated lower in their first two years (Bidwell 2011). Verify the 18% figure.
- Referred hires tend to stay longer (Burks et al. 2015).
- Language models on application text can raise prediction and cut group differences when designed with care (Campion et al. 2024).

## Turnover

Holtom, Mitchell, Lee & Inderrieden (2005): shocks, not slowly building dissatisfaction, start most voluntary leaving. Mitchell et al. (2001): job embeddedness predicts staying.
